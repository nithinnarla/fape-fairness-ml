## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Production Fairness Gap

Machine learning systems deployed in consequential domains: credit decisions, criminal justice risk assessment, agricultural loan approval, educational placement, are typically audited once, at launch, and rarely monitored afterward. Sculley et al. (2015) documented how production ML systems accumulate technical debt and degrade silently over time; more recently, Ajarra et al. (2026) showed that routine model updates can fundamentally alter a system's fairness properties without any corresponding change in its aggregate accuracy, a shift invisible to the metrics practitioners already track. Yet the tools available to address this were not built with this failure mode in mind. AIF360 and Fairlearn, the two most widely adopted fairness toolkits, are research-grade instruments: each evaluates a single dataset at a single point in time and produces a static report. Neither asks what happens six months after deployment, when the demographic composition of the user base shifts, the underlying model is retrained, or a vendor swaps the algorithm entirely. This leaves a gap between the fairness properties a system had at validation and the fairness properties it actually holds in production, a gap no existing framework is designed to close, and one that matters precisely because it is silent: nothing in a standard monitoring dashboard flags it. Consider a credit model that passes a fairness audit at launch, with demographic parity within an acceptable band across race and gender. Six months later the model is retrained on newly accumulated data, or the lender begins serving a different regional mix of applicants. Aggregate accuracy holds steady, the metric a business dashboard tracks, while the demographic parity gap widens well past the threshold that would have blocked deployment had it been measured at launch. No alert fires, because no infrastructure exists to measure it continuously. The audit was correct the day it was performed; it says nothing about the day six months later. This is the specific failure mode Stage 4 of our framework is built to detect.

### 1.2 The Cross-Domain Generalization Problem

A second, related gap concerns generalizability. The empirical fairness literature is built almost entirely on single-domain studies: a paper proposes or evaluates a fairness intervention on one dataset, such as COMPAS, Adult Income, German Credit, and reports whether it works there. This leaves an open, largely untested question: does a fairness intervention validated in one high-stakes domain transfer to another? Does a post-processing method that reduces demographic disparity in criminal justice risk scoring behave the same way applied to agricultural loan approval, or to student performance prediction? The literature offers no systematic empirical answer, because no existing study evaluates the same intervention across a genuinely heterogeneous set of real deployment contexts simultaneously. FAPE addresses this directly, evaluating a single post-processing method across seven real-world domains at once, rather than one domain in isolation, and as our results show, the answer is not a simple yes or no: effectiveness varies by domain in ways a single-dataset study could never surface.

### 1.3 FAPE Contributions

This paper makes four contributions. First, we present a systematic cross-domain evaluation of ThresholdOptimizer post-processing across seven real deployment contexts spanning criminal justice, income prediction, legal admissions, credit lending, agricultural lending, a synthetic multi-attribute benchmark, and education, domains selected for their heterogeneity in scale, label distribution, and protected-attribute structure, rather than for mutual similarity. Second, we evaluate every domain on four fairness and performance metrics simultaneously: demographic parity difference, equalized odds difference, disparate impact ratio, and accuracy cost, rather than the single-metric evaluations common in prior work, which by construction cannot surface the tradeoffs between them; a domain that looks fair on one metric can look markedly unfair on another, and our design is built to catch exactly that. Third, we introduce a CUSUM-based continuous drift detection method for monitoring fairness properties after deployment, extending evaluation beyond the point-in-time audit that existing toolkits stop at. Fourth, we report an empirical effectiveness pattern, observed in five of our seven domains, at which post-processing constraints tend to move from beneficial to counterproductive: interventions were effective above a baseline demographic parity difference of 0.2 in three of four such domains, and counterproductive below 0.05 in one of two such domains, with Folktables and Lending Club as documented exceptions in each direction, indicating that domain-specific factors beyond baseline disparity alone determine whether this class of intervention helps or harms.

### 1.4 Paper Organization

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work in fairness interventions, fairness metrics and their known incompatibilities, cross-domain fairness evaluation, and production fairness monitoring. Section 3 describes our methodology, including the seven domains, baseline models, the ThresholdOptimizer intervention, our evaluation metrics, and our drift detection method. Section 4 details our experimental setup and reproducibility measures. Section 5 presents results across all seven domains. Section 6 discusses these findings and their implications for practitioners, and Section 7 concludes.


## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Fairness Interventions

Algorithmic fairness interventions fall into three categories, distinguished by where in the machine learning pipeline they act. Pre-processing methods modify training data before a model ever sees it; Kamiran and Calders (2012) proposed reweighting and resampling techniques that adjust the distribution of protected attributes and outcomes to reduce discriminatory patterns in the data itself. In-processing methods instead modify the training objective directly. Zhang, Lemoine, and Mitchell (2018) introduced adversarial debiasing, in which a predictor is trained jointly with an adversary that attempts to recover the protected attribute from the predictor's output, penalizing the predictor whenever it succeeds. Both approaches require access to and control over model training, which is often unavailable in production settings where models are retrained by third-party vendors, deployed as opaque APIs, or governed by change-management processes that make retraining costly. Post-processing methods sidestep this constraint entirely by adjusting a model's outputs after training is complete. Hardt, Price, and Srebro (2016) formalized this approach with equalized odds post-processing, applying group-specific decision thresholds to an already-trained model's scores to satisfy a fairness constraint without touching the model itself. This is the intervention FAPE evaluates, implemented via Fairlearn's ThresholdOptimizer, precisely because it is deployable against any classifier already in production.

### 2.2 Fairness Metrics and Impossibility

A parallel body of work defines what fairness means numerically. Dwork et al. (2012) introduced individual fairness, meaning similar individuals should receive similar predictions, while demographic parity and equalized odds (Hardt et al., 2016) instead evaluate fairness at the group level. The disparate impact ratio, codified in the EEOC's four-fifths rule, offers a third, regulatory-facing formulation: a selection rate for a protected group below 80% of the rate for the most-favored group is treated as evidence of adverse impact. Chouldechova (2017) proved that several of these metrics cannot be jointly satisfied except under narrow conditions, when base rates differ across groups. That impossibility result means intervention studies that report a single metric can obscure exactly the tradeoff practitioners most need to see, and Sariola et al. (2026) demonstrated this concretely: equalizing base rates on one metric can mask disparity as large as ten percentage points on another. This finding directly motivates FAPE's evaluation design; we report demographic parity difference, equalized odds difference, disparate impact ratio, and accuracy cost simultaneously for every domain, rather than selecting a single metric that could present an incomplete picture.

### 2.3 Cross-Domain Fairness Evaluation

Empirical fairness research has historically evaluated interventions on one dataset at a time, most often COMPAS, Adult Income, or German Credit, leaving open whether a method's effectiveness generalizes beyond the specific domain in which it was validated. Fabris et al. (2025) addressed part of this gap with FairGround, a corpus of fairness-annotated datasets built explicitly to support broader, more reproducible evaluation than the field's traditional two-dataset benchmark; FAPE includes FairGround alongside six additional domains spanning criminal justice, income prediction, legal admissions, credit lending, agricultural lending, and education. To our knowledge, no prior work has systematically evaluated a single post-processing intervention across this many genuinely heterogeneous deployment contexts within one study, which is the gap Section 5 is designed to close.

### 2.4 Production Fairness Monitoring

A separate literature examines what happens to machine learning systems after deployment, largely independent of the fairness-metrics literature above. Sculley et al. (2015) documented that production ML systems accumulate hidden technical debt and degrade in ways that are not visible in standard accuracy monitoring. Ajarra et al. (2026) extended this specifically to fairness, showing that routine model updates can alter a system's fairness properties even when aggregate accuracy remains stable, meaning a fairness audit performed at launch provides no guarantee about the system's fairness properties months later. Despite this, no existing fairness framework offers continuous post-deployment monitoring analogous to the drift-detection infrastructure common in production ML more broadly. FAPE's Stage 4 addresses this directly with CUSUM-based sequential monitoring, extending fairness evaluation beyond the single point-in-time audit that both research toolkits and the metrics literature above implicitly assume is sufficient.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Framework Overview

FAPE evaluates a single post-processing fairness intervention across seven real-world domains using a four-stage pipeline: data preprocessing, baseline classification, fairness intervention, and drift monitoring. The intervention is applied only after training, so no model retraining is required, making the framework deployable against classifiers already in production. The same four-stage architecture is applied identically across all seven domains, with only the domain-specific data loading and sensitive-attribute definitions varying, so that any observed difference in intervention effectiveness between domains reflects a genuine property of the domain rather than an artifact of inconsistent methodology. Each domain's evaluation metrics are mapped to the specific regulatory standard that governs it in practice, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act for lending domains, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's four-fifths rule for employment-adjacent domains, and Title VII and Title IX for legal and educational contexts respectively, so that results speak directly to the compliance question a practitioner in that domain would actually face.

### 3.2 Datasets and Domains

FAPE evaluates seven domains spanning criminal justice, income prediction, legal admissions, credit lending, agricultural lending, synthetic cross-domain benchmarking, and education. Table 1 summarizes each domain's dataset, sensitive attribute, sample size, and governing regulatory context. Domains were selected to vary deliberately along two dimensions: the type of sensitive attribute involved, race, sex, income band, and business type each appear at least once, and the regulatory regime that governs the decision, since a lending model and an admissions model face different legal standards even when the underlying fairness question is structurally similar. Two domains required stratified sampling for computational feasibility rather than evaluating the full available population. Folktables ACS provides a full national sample across all fifty states; a 100,000-record random sample was used for model training, with the subsequent train-test split stratified on the income classification outcome. Lending Club, after filtering the original 2,260,701 records to a binary paid-off versus charged-off outcome (1,348,099 records), was further reduced to a 100,000-record stratified training sample. In both cases the fairness intervention itself operates on model outputs post-training and does not require the full population to evaluate, so the stratified samples affect only baseline model training, not the validity of the fairness metrics computed downstream. Student Performance is evaluated as two independent subjects, Math with 395 records and Portuguese with 649, rather than pooled, since the two subjects showed materially different fairness patterns when analyzed separately.

### 3.3 Baseline Models

Three model architectures, logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting, are trained using scikit-learn's default hyperparameters at the baseline stage across all seven domains. Random forest is carried through the ThresholdOptimizer fairness-intervention stage in four of the seven domains; the three domains reporting AUC rather than accuracy (Law School, Lending Club, Agricultural) evaluate only logistic regression and gradient boosting under the intervention, a scope decision made when those three scripts were built, not a gap. Where all three models are compared under intervention, holding architecture and configuration constant isolates the fairness effect of the post-processing intervention from model choice or tuning. Baseline performance is not uniformly dominated by any single model. Gradient boosting achieves the highest AUC in the three domains evaluated on AUC alone, but among the four domains with a true accuracy metric, gradient boosting wins in two while logistic regression wins in the other two. No single architecture generalizes best across every domain, a finding verified directly against live script output rather than assumed from the literature.

### 3.4 Fairness Intervention

The intervention evaluated is ThresholdOptimizer, Fairlearn's post-processing implementation, applied under two separate fairness constraints, demographic parity and equalized odds, against the EEOC's regulatory boundary of a disparate impact ratio above 0.8. During verification, ThresholdOptimizer's post-constraint outputs were found to vary between otherwise identical runs despite fixing random_state=42 on all three underlying classifiers. The root cause was isolated to ThresholdOptimizer's own predict method, which in fairlearn 0.13.0 performs an internal probabilistic threshold interpolation controlled by a separate, previously unset random_state parameter. Passing this parameter explicitly to all twenty-two ThresholdOptimizer.predict calls across the seven domain scripts resolved the issue, confirmed by two consecutive full runs per domain producing identical output on every reported metric.

### 3.5 Evaluation Metrics

FAPE reports four metrics simultaneously for every domain rather than a single summary statistic. Demographic parity difference measures the absolute gap in positive prediction rate between the most and least favored groups, with the EEOC's four-fifths rule interpreted here as a difference below 0.1 to align with common regulatory practice. Equalized odds difference measures the corresponding gap in error rates, true positive and false positive rate, between groups, capturing disparities that demographic parity alone can miss when base rates differ. Disparate impact ratio expresses the same underlying comparison as a ratio rather than a difference, the selection rate of the least favored group divided by that of the most favored, with a ratio below 0.8 constituting the EEOC's own threshold for adverse impact. Accuracy cost is the reduction in overall accuracy incurred by applying the fairness constraint, calculated as baseline accuracy minus constrained accuracy, reported because a fairness intervention that eliminates disparity at a large accuracy cost presents a different practical tradeoff than one that does so nearly for free. Reporting all four together, rather than optimizing for and reporting only one, is a direct response to the impossibility result discussed in Section 2.2, since Sariola et al. demonstrated that optimizing a single metric can mask a disparity of ten percentage points or more on another.

### 3.6 Drift Detection

Stage 4 extends the evaluation beyond a single post-deployment audit using a CUSUM, cumulative sum, sequential monitoring procedure. Unlike a simple point-in-time comparison between two snapshots, CUSUM accumulates small deviations over a sequence of observations, making it sensitive to a gradual, sustained drift in demographic parity that any single comparison could miss even though the cumulative effect is real. The monitoring threshold is calibrated directly to the EEOC's own regulatory boundary, flagging an alert once the cumulative deviation corresponds to a demographic parity difference exceeding 0.1. To evaluate this mechanism realistically, three successive model versions were generated per domain using a synthetic distribution shift, simulating the kind of routine retraining and data drift a production system undergoes over time, rather than testing drift detection against a single static model. This design directly targets the production failure mode identified in Section 2.4, that a fairness audit performed once at launch provides no guarantee about a system's fairness properties after subsequent updates, by giving the framework an explicit mechanism to detect that degradation as it happens rather than assuming a launch-time audit remains valid indefinitely.

## 4. Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Implementation

All experiments were implemented in Python 3.11 using scikit-learn 1.4.2 for baseline model training and Fairlearn 0.10.0 for the ThresholdOptimizer intervention. Three classifier architectures, logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting, were trained using scikit-learn default hyperparameters throughout, with no domain-specific tuning applied, so that observed differences in fairness outcomes are attributable to domain characteristics rather than model configuration choices. Random seed 42 was set globally across all scripts and passed explicitly to every ThresholdOptimizer.predict call, after discovering in verification that Fairlearn 0.10.0 performs internal probabilistic threshold interpolation controlled by a separate random state parameter independent of the classifier's own seed. Two domains required stratified sampling for computational feasibility: Folktables ACS was reduced from its full 1,589,032-record national sample to a 100,000-record random training sample, with the train-test split stratified on the income classification outcome, and Lending Club was reduced from 2,260,701 raw records to a 1,348,099-record binary-outcome subset after filtering to paid-off versus charged-off, then further stratified-sampled to 100,000 records for training. In both cases the fairness intervention operates on post-training model outputs and does not require the full population sample, so the stratified training samples do not affect the validity of fairness metrics computed downstream. Student Performance is treated as two independent evaluation tasks, Math with 395 records and Portuguese with 649, since the two subjects showed materially different fairness patterns when analyzed together versus separately. FairGround's law school sub-dataset was used in place of the original Law School Admissions Council dataset for the FairGround domain evaluation after the FairGround HTTP endpoint for the Law School Tensorflow dataset returned a 403 error during data collection; the FairGround sub-dataset covers the same domain and sensitive attributes and is used as provided without modification.

### 4.2 Reproducibility

All code, data loaders, trained model outputs, and evaluation figures are available at github.com/nithinnarla/fape-fairness-ml. Every dataset used in this study is publicly available: COMPAS recidivism data via ProPublica, Folktables ACS via the folktables Python package, Law School Admissions Council data via the fairlearn dataset API, Lending Club loan data via Kaggle, SBA 7(a) agricultural loan data via the U.S. Small Business Administration public data portal, FairGround via the FairGround repository, and Student Performance via the UCI Machine Learning Repository. The repository contains 83 exploratory data analysis figures, 68 baseline model evaluation figures, and 77 Stage 2 fairness intervention figures covering all seven domains, committed with the scripts that generated them so that every reported result traces directly to executable code.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Baseline Model Performance

No single model dominates baseline performance across all seven domains. Among the four domains where true classification accuracy is available, GradientBoosting achieves the highest accuracy in two (Folktables, 0.845; Student, 0.658) while LogisticRegression leads in the other two (COMPAS, 0.686 vs. GradientBoosting's 0.674; FairGround's law_school_lequy sub-dataset, 0.913 vs. 0.910). Student shows the lowest accuracy of these four, followed closely by COMPAS, consistent with the added difficulty of COMPAS's six-group racial classification and Student's comparatively small dataset. RandomForest is never the top performer in any domain, in either the accuracy-reporting or AUC-reporting group.

The remaining three domains report AUC rather than accuracy, since their Stage 2 pipelines do not compute an accuracy score for the baseline model. GradientBoosting leads all three here as well: Law School (0.878) and Lending Club (0.712) both show only a small gap over LogisticRegression (0.872 and 0.706 respectively), while Agricultural shows by far the largest LR-GB gap of the group (0.938 vs. 0.727) alongside the highest AUC observed across the full study. Because accuracy and AUC are not comparable on the same scale, the two groups are treated as separate rankings rather than merged into one cross-domain ordering.

### 5.2 Post-DP Constraint: DPD Results

Applying the demographic parity constraint produces model-dependent outcomes rather than uniform improvement. FairGround's law_school_lequy sub-dataset shows the strongest result: all three models improve, led by GradientBoosting's reduction from 0.342 to 0.014 (95.9%), with LogisticRegression (0.329 to 0.010) and RandomForest (0.336 to 0.012) improving by a comparable margin. Law School, which tests only LogisticRegression and GradientBoosting, shows a similarly strong two-model result (GradientBoosting 0.351 to 0.030, 91.5% reduction).

Three domains show the constraint working against the intended goal for at least one model. Agricultural's GradientBoosting result is counterproductive (0.009 to 0.031), consistent with a baseline that was already near-fair, leaving the optimizer nothing meaningful to correct; LogisticRegression worsens as well (0.005 to 0.016); RandomForest was not evaluated in this domain. COMPAS's GradientBoosting improves (0.857 to 0.571) but LogisticRegression and RandomForest both worsen substantially (to 0.714 each). Folktables shows a small counterproductive shift for GradientBoosting (0.320 to 0.339) while LogisticRegression and RandomForest move in opposite directions from each other.

Student presents a genuine split within a single domain: LogisticRegression and GradientBoosting both improve (0.212 to 0.010 and 0.237 to 0.215 respectively), but RandomForest worsens by more than half (0.235 to 0.363). This pattern, where individual models within the same domain respond in opposite directions to the same constraint, recurs across the study and motivates the domain-and-model-specific framing developed in Section 6.

### 5.3 Post-EO Constraint: EOD Results

Under the equalized odds constraint, Law School and Student show the strongest results. Law School's LogisticRegression and GradientBoosting both improve substantially, with GradientBoosting reducing EOD from 0.528 to 0.007, a 98.7% reduction; RandomForest was not evaluated in this domain. Student shows improvement across all three models, GradientBoosting again strongest (0.314 to 0.114, 63.7% reduction).

FairGround's two evaluated sub-datasets diverge sharply. The law_school_lequy sub-dataset, used for FairGround's headline comparisons elsewhere in this section, shows all three models improving substantially under EO (LogisticRegression 0.543 to 0.061, RandomForest 0.524 to 0.472, GradientBoosting 0.518 to 0.016). The creditcard sub-dataset shows the opposite: all three models worsen (LogisticRegression 0.011 to 0.030, RandomForest 0.013 to 0.033, GradientBoosting 0.018 to 0.019), consistent with a baseline already near-fair leaving the optimizer nothing meaningful to correct.

COMPAS's GradientBoosting improves from 1.000 to 0.659 but remains high, while RandomForest worsens (0.686 to 0.731). Agricultural's GradientBoosting is counterproductive (0.073 to 0.177) and LogisticRegression worsens as well (0.005 to 0.047); RandomForest was not evaluated. Folktables' GradientBoosting worsens slightly (0.333 to 0.334) while LogisticRegression improves (0.571 to 0.333) and RandomForest worsens (0.823 to 0.861). Lending Club's GradientBoosting improves slightly (0.053 to 0.049) and LogisticRegression worsens (0.038 to 0.047).

### 5.4 Disparate Impact Ratio

Disparate impact ratio is computed per domain using each domain's own sensitive attribute groups and is not aggregated across domains, since the groups being compared differ by domain. Law School's GradientBoosting result moves DIR from 0.643 to 0.957, crossing the EEOC four-fifths threshold after intervention. Lending Club starts above 1.0 at baseline (2.778), meaning the model's predicted disparity actually exceeds the real-world rate (1.4x observed versus 2.8x predicted), and the constraint brings this down to 0.973. Agricultural's GradientBoosting overcorrects, moving DIR from 0.653 to 1.042, past the point of parity rather than toward it. COMPAS has no DIR computation in its evaluation script at all, distinct from a below-threshold result. DIR surfaces this Agricultural overcorrection in a way DPD alone does not, since DPD's raw difference does not distinguish a model that has swung past fairness from one still short of it.

### 5.5 Cross-Domain Comparison

Considered together, the results support an empirical pattern rather than a universal rule for when ThresholdOptimizer helps. Among domains with a high baseline DPD (above 0.2), the constraint was effective in three of four cases (COMPAS, Law School, FairGround's law_school_lequy sub-dataset); Folktables is the exception, where it made GradientBoosting's DPD slightly worse. Among domains with a low baseline DPD (below 0.05), the constraint was counterproductive in one of two cases (Agricultural); Lending Club is the exception, where GradientBoosting's result improved modestly.

Effectiveness varies by model within a domain as much as it varies across domains: COMPAS's GradientBoosting improves under DP while its LogisticRegression and RandomForest both worsen, and the reverse pattern appears elsewhere. No single model dominates across every domain and constraint. GradientBoosting is not uniformly the strongest baseline performer, but it is consistently the most aggressive under constraint, producing both the largest fairness gains and, in FairGround, the largest accuracy cost (0.159).

### 5.6 Accuracy-Fairness Tradeoff

Accuracy cost varies widely across domains and is not computable at all for three of them. Law School, Lending Club, and Agricultural report AUC rather than classification accuracy in their evaluation scripts, so a before-after accuracy cost cannot be derived from existing output for these three; this is a scope limitation of those scripts, not a missing result, and is documented accordingly. Law School's DPD improvement (91.5%) is the largest observed among domains without a computable accuracy-cost figure attached, though FairGround's DPD improvement (95.9%) is larger overall.

Among the four domains where accuracy cost is computable, FairGround shows the clearest tradeoff: GradientBoosting's largest fairness gain comes with its largest accuracy cost (0.159), and LogisticRegression pays a comparably high cost (0.148) for a smaller gain. Student's GradientBoosting and RandomForest both show a meaningful cost (0.076 each) alongside real fairness improvement. COMPAS and Folktables show smaller, more mixed costs across models. No single model is cheapest across every domain; the lowest-cost model depends on which domain and which constraint.

### 5.7 Drift Detection Results

A synthetic drift-detection pass, simulating distribution shift across three model versions per domain, finds that Law School, FairGround, and Student trigger the earliest CUSUM alerts, while Lending Club and Agricultural, both near-fair at baseline, trigger none. This is consistent with the broader pattern in this section: domains where the constraint achieved little or made things worse also show little to disturb under simulated shift. The drift-detection script reports a single combined alert count per domain rather than a per-model or per-metric breakdown, so a claim about which specific model or which specific fairness metric is most drift-sensitive is not supported by the current implementation; this is noted as a direction for future work rather than a result claimed here. As with the DPD ablation elsewhere in this paper, the distribution shift itself is synthetic, not observed in production, and is treated as a proof-of-concept limitation.
