## Abstract

Fairness audits in production ML typically occur once, at deployment, on a single domain. Both fail in practice: fairness can shift after retraining or a changing user base, and interventions validated on one dataset are rarely tested across the heterogeneous domains an organization actually deploys. We present FAPE (Fairness Auditing in Production Environments), a four-stage framework evaluating a single post-processing intervention, Fairlearn's ThresholdOptimizer, across eight real-world domain evaluations: criminal justice, income prediction, legal admissions, credit lending, agricultural lending, a synthetic multi-attribute benchmark, healthcare, and education. Every domain is scored on demographic parity and equalized odds difference, plus disparate impact ratio and accuracy cost where computable, unlike the single-metric evaluations common in prior work. A CUSUM-based drift-monitoring stage extends evaluation past the point-in-time audit existing toolkits stop at. Intervention effectiveness depends on baseline disparity magnitude: across model-domain pairs the constraint improved disparity in 9 of 14 high-disparity cases and worsened it in 3 of 4 near-fair ones. Domains with the largest gains were also where simulated post-deployment drift was detected soonest. A single deployment-time audit is therefore an unreliable guide to an intervention's real-world behavior, arguing for baseline-disparity screening and continuous monitoring as standard practice.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Production Fairness Gap

Machine learning systems deployed in consequential domains: credit decisions, criminal justice risk assessment, agricultural loan approval, educational placement, are typically audited once, at launch, and rarely monitored afterward. Sculley et al. (2015) documented how production ML systems accumulate technical debt and degrade silently over time; more recently, Ajarra and Basu (2026) showed that routine model updates can fundamentally alter a system's fairness properties without any corresponding change in its aggregate accuracy, a shift invisible to the metrics practitioners already track. The tools available to address this were not built with this failure mode in mind, however. AIF360 and Fairlearn are the two most widely adopted fairness toolkits, and both remain research-grade instruments in one specific sense: each produces a static report from a single evaluation at a single point in time. Neither tool asks what happens six months later, and none of what happens then is visible to a framework built only to check fairness once: the user base shifts, the model is retrained, or a vendor swaps the algorithm out entirely. What results is a gap between the fairness properties a system had at validation and the fairness properties it actually holds in production right now, a gap no existing framework is designed to close. Nothing in a standard monitoring dashboard flags the gap, which is what makes it dangerous. Consider a credit model that passes a fairness audit at launch with demographic parity within an acceptable band across race and gender. Six months later it is retrained on new data, or the lender begins serving a different regional mix of applicants. Aggregate accuracy holds steady, the metric a business dashboard tracks, while the demographic parity gap widens past the threshold that would have blocked deployment at launch. No alert fires, because nothing measures it continuously. Stage 4 is built to detect this.

### 1.2 The Cross-Domain Generalization Problem

A second, related gap concerns generalizability. A pattern runs through the empirical fairness literature: nearly every study evaluates its intervention on a single domain. A paper proposes or tests a method on one dataset, COMPAS, Adult Income, German Credit, and reports whether it worked there. Whether that same intervention would hold up in a different high-stakes domain is a question the field has largely left untested. Does a post-processing method that reduces demographic disparity in criminal justice risk scoring behave the same way applied to agricultural loan approval, or to student performance prediction? The literature offers no systematic empirical answer that we are aware of, because no existing study evaluates the same intervention across a genuinely heterogeneous set of real deployment contexts simultaneously. FAPE addresses this directly, evaluating a single post-processing method across eight real-world evaluations at once, rather than one domain in isolation, and as our results show, the answer is not a simple yes or no: effectiveness varies by domain in ways a single-dataset study could never surface.

### 1.3 FAPE Contributions

This paper makes four contributions. First, we present a systematic cross-domain evaluation of ThresholdOptimizer post-processing across eight deployment-context evaluations spanning criminal justice, income prediction, legal admissions, credit lending, agricultural lending, a synthetic multi-attribute benchmark, healthcare, and education, domains selected for their heterogeneity in scale, label distribution, and protected-attribute structure. Second, we evaluate each domain on multiple fairness and performance metrics at once, demographic parity difference and equalized odds difference throughout, plus disparate impact ratio and accuracy cost where the domain's pipeline computes them, rather than the single-metric evaluations common in prior work, which by construction cannot surface the tradeoffs between them; a domain that looks fair on one metric can look markedly unfair on another, and our design is built to catch exactly that. Third, we introduce a CUSUM-based continuous drift detection method for monitoring fairness properties after deployment, extending evaluation beyond the point-in-time audit that existing toolkits stop at. Fourth, we report an empirical effectiveness pattern, observed across all eight evaluations, at which post-processing constraints tend to move from beneficial to counterproductive: counting every model-domain pair, the constraint improved disparity in 9 of the 14 pairs with a baseline demographic parity difference above 0.2, or 6 to 7 of 11 to 12 once the duplicated evaluation of Section 6.4 is counted once, and worsened it in 3 of the 4 pairs below 0.05, indicating that factors beyond baseline disparity alone determine whether this class of intervention helps or harms.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Fairness Interventions

Algorithmic fairness interventions fall into three categories, distinguished by where in the machine learning pipeline they act. Pre-processing methods modify training data before a model ever sees it; Kamiran and Calders (2012) proposed reweighting and resampling techniques that adjust the distribution of protected attributes and outcomes to reduce discriminatory patterns in the data itself. In-processing methods instead modify the training objective directly. Zhang et al. (2018) introduced adversarial debiasing: a predictor trained jointly with an adversary that tries to recover the protected attribute from the predictor's output, with the predictor penalized each time the adversary succeeds. Both pre-processing and in-processing share the same practical limitation: they need direct access to the training pipeline. In most production settings that access is not available. A vendor retrains on its own internal schedule, an API returns predictions with no visibility into how the model was built, and change-management can turn a routine retraining into a multi-week approval cycle. Post-processing methods avoid this problem, since they work on a model's outputs after training has already finished. Hardt et al. (2016) formalized this approach with equalized odds post-processing, applying group-specific decision thresholds to an already-trained model's scores to satisfy a fairness constraint without touching the model itself. This is the intervention FAPE evaluates, implemented via Fairlearn's ThresholdOptimizer, precisely because it is deployable against any classifier already in production.

### 2.2 Fairness Metrics and Impossibility

A parallel body of work defines what fairness means numerically. Dwork et al. (2012) introduced individual fairness: similar individuals should receive similar predictions. Demographic parity and equalized odds (Hardt et al., 2016) take a different approach: they evaluate fairness at the group level, not the individual level. A third formulation, the disparate impact ratio, grew out of the EEOC's four-fifths rule in employment discrimination law and has since become a general convention across fairness research more broadly. Under this rule, a selection rate for a protected group below 80% of the rate for the most-favored group counts as evidence of adverse impact. Chouldechova (2017) proved that several of these metrics cannot be jointly satisfied except under narrow conditions, when base rates differ across groups. That impossibility result means intervention studies that report a single metric can obscure exactly the tradeoff practitioners most need to see, and Sariola et al. (2026) demonstrated this concretely: equalizing base rates on one metric can mask disparity as large as ten percentage points on another. This finding directly motivates FAPE's evaluation design; we report demographic parity difference and equalized odds difference for every domain, alongside disparate impact ratio and accuracy cost where available, rather than selecting a single metric that could present an incomplete picture.

### 2.3 Cross-Domain Fairness Evaluation

Empirical fairness research has historically evaluated interventions on one dataset at a time, most often COMPAS, Adult Income, or German Credit, leaving open whether a method's effectiveness generalizes beyond the specific domain in which it was validated. Simson et al. (2025) addressed part of this gap with FairGround, a corpus of fairness-annotated datasets built explicitly to support broader, more reproducible evaluation than the field's traditional two-dataset benchmark; FAPE includes FairGround alongside six additional domains spanning criminal justice, income prediction, legal admissions, credit lending, agricultural lending, and education. To our knowledge, no prior work has systematically evaluated a single post-processing intervention across this many distinct deployment contexts within one study; Section 6.4 states precisely how many independent data sources the eight evaluations rest on. Closing that gap is what Section 5 is designed to do.

### 2.4 Production Fairness Monitoring

A separate literature examines what happens to machine learning systems after deployment, largely independent of the fairness-metrics literature above. Sculley et al. (2015) documented that production ML systems accumulate hidden technical debt and degrade in ways that are not visible in standard accuracy monitoring. Ajarra and Basu (2026) extended this specifically to fairness, showing that routine model updates can alter a system's fairness properties even when aggregate accuracy remains stable, meaning a fairness audit performed at launch provides no guarantee about the system's fairness properties months later. Despite this, no existing fairness framework offers continuous post-deployment monitoring analogous to the drift-detection infrastructure common in production ML more broadly; Breck et al.'s (2017) 28-test production readiness rubric, for instance, includes no fairness-specific tests at all. FAPE's Stage 4 addresses this directly with CUSUM-based sequential monitoring, extending fairness evaluation beyond the single point-in-time audit that both research toolkits and the metrics literature above implicitly assume is sufficient.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Framework Overview

![Figure 1: FAPE's four-stage pipeline](../figures/paper/fape_pipeline_diagram.png)

Figure 1 shows the pipeline used throughout. FAPE runs a single post-processing fairness intervention across eight real-world evaluations in four stages: data preprocessing, baseline classification, fairness intervention, and drift monitoring. The intervention touches a model only after training, so no retraining is needed, which makes the framework usable against classifiers already in production. Every domain goes through the identical four-stage architecture; only data loading and the sensitive-attribute definition change. Holding the architecture constant means differences across domains trace to the domain, not to inconsistent processing. Where a domain-specific standard applies, results are framed against it: ECOA's individual-applicant provisions for Lending Club and its business-credit provisions for Agricultural (Section 6.4). Outside the two lending domains, the 0.8 disparate impact ratio threshold is applied as a general fairness-research convention rather than a compliance test, consistent with Section 2.2. Federal civil-rights statutes may still govern deployed systems in these domains, Title VI and Title IX for federally funded education and Affordable Care Act Section 1557 for federally funded health programs among them. Determining whether a given deployment falls under them is a legal question this study does not attempt to settle.

### 3.3 Baseline Models

Logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting are trained with scikit-learn's default hyperparameters at the baseline stage across all eight evaluations. Random forest is carried through the ThresholdOptimizer fairness-intervention stage in five of the eight; the three domains reporting AUC rather than accuracy (Law School, Lending Club, Agricultural) evaluate only logistic regression and gradient boosting under the intervention, a scope decision made when those three scripts were built. Where all three are compared under intervention, holding architecture and configuration constant isolates the intervention's fairness effect from model choice or tuning. Baseline performance is not uniformly dominated by any single model. Gradient boosting achieves the highest AUC in the three domains evaluated on AUC alone, but among the five evaluations with a true accuracy metric, gradient boosting wins in three while logistic regression wins in the other two. No single architecture generalizes best across every domain, verified against live script output.

### 3.4 Fairness Intervention

The intervention evaluated is ThresholdOptimizer, Fairlearn's post-processing implementation, applied under two separate fairness constraints, demographic parity and equalized odds, against the EEOC's regulatory boundary of a disparate impact ratio above 0.8. During verification, its post-constraint outputs varied between otherwise identical runs, despite fixing random_state=42 on all three underlying classifiers. The cause turned out to be internal to ThresholdOptimizer itself: fairlearn 0.13.0's predict method performs a probabilistic threshold interpolation controlled by its own random_state parameter, left unset. Passing it explicitly to all twenty-two ThresholdOptimizer.predict calls fixed the issue, confirmed by two consecutive full runs per domain returning identical output on every metric.

### 3.5 Evaluation Metrics

FAPE reports up to four metrics per domain. Demographic parity difference and equalized odds difference are computed for all eight; disparate impact ratio and accuracy cost are computed where each domain's Stage 2 pipeline supports them, and Section 5 states the coverage for each. Demographic parity difference measures the absolute gap in positive prediction rate between the most and least favored groups; following common practice in the fairness literature, a difference below 0.1 is interpreted as broadly acceptable, treated as a research convention rather than a binding legal standard for any domain in this study. Equalized odds difference measures the corresponding gap in error rates, true positive and false positive rate, between groups, capturing disparities that demographic parity alone can miss when base rates differ. Disparate impact ratio expresses the same underlying comparison as a ratio rather than a difference. Each domain that reports one computes it as the selection rate of a designated group divided by that of a designated reference group, with the pair fixed in advance rather than chosen as the observed minimum and maximum: minority over majority for Law School, partnership over corporation for Agricultural, and lowest income quartile over highest for Lending Club. A ratio below 0.8 is applied as the convention described in Sections 2.2 and 3.1, and treated as a binding threshold only for Lending Club and Agricultural, where ECOA governs directly. Because the pair is fixed, a ratio above 1.0 does not indicate compliance; it indicates that the designated group is selected more often than the reference group, which is disparity in the opposite direction. The threshold is therefore one-sided, and Section 5.4 reads values above 1.0 accordingly. Accuracy cost is the reduction in overall accuracy incurred by applying the fairness constraint, calculated as baseline accuracy minus constrained accuracy, reported because eliminating disparity at a large accuracy cost is a different practical tradeoff than doing so nearly for free. Reporting these together, rather than optimizing for and reporting only one, is a direct response to the impossibility result discussed in Section 2.2, since Sariola et al. demonstrated that optimizing a single metric can mask a disparity of ten percentage points or more on another.

### 3.6 Drift Detection

Stage 4 extends the evaluation beyond a single post-deployment audit using a CUSUM, cumulative sum, sequential monitoring procedure. Unlike a point-in-time comparison between two snapshots, CUSUM accumulates small deviations across a sequence of observations, making it sensitive to gradual, sustained drift in demographic parity that any single comparison would miss. The monitoring threshold is calibrated to the EEOC boundary, flagging an alert once cumulative deviation corresponds to a demographic parity difference above 0.1. Three successive model versions were generated per domain using a synthetic distribution shift, simulating the routine retraining and data drift a production system undergoes. This targets the failure mode identified in Section 2.4, giving the framework an explicit mechanism to detect post-deployment degradation as it happens.

## 4. Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Implementation

All experiments were implemented in Python 3.11.9, using scikit-learn 1.9.1 for baseline model training and Fairlearn 0.13.0 for the ThresholdOptimizer intervention. Three classifier architectures were trained throughout: logistic regression, random forest, and gradient boosting, each using scikit-learn's default hyperparameters. No domain-specific tuning was applied, a deliberate constraint keeping observed fairness differences attributable to the domains rather than to model configuration. Random seed 42 was set globally across all scripts and passed explicitly to every ThresholdOptimizer.predict call, for the reason given in Section 3.4. The stratified sampling applied to Folktables ACS and Lending Club, and the separate treatment of Student Performance's two subjects, are described in Section 3.2. FairGround's law school sub-dataset was used in place of the original Law School Admissions Council dataset for the FairGround domain evaluation after the FairGround HTTP endpoint for the Law School Tensorflow dataset returned a 403 error during data collection; the FairGround sub-dataset covers the same domain and sensitive attributes and is used as provided without modification.

### 4.2 Reproducibility

All code, data loaders, trained model outputs, and evaluation figures are available at github.com/nithinnarla/fape-fairness-ml. Every dataset used in this study is publicly available: COMPAS recidivism data via ProPublica (Angwin et al., 2016), Folktables ACS via the folktables Python package, Law School Admissions Council data via the fairlearn dataset API, Lending Club loan data via Kaggle, SBA 7(a) agricultural loan data via the U.S. Small Business Administration public data portal, FairGround via the FairGround repository, and Student Performance via the UCI Machine Learning Repository. The repository contains 83 exploratory data analysis figures, 68 baseline model evaluation figures, and 77 Stage 2 fairness intervention figures covering all seven domain scripts, committed with the scripts that generated them so that every reported result traces directly to executable code. The exact package versions used are pinned in requirements.txt: Python 3.11.9, scikit-learn 1.9.1, Fairlearn 0.13.0, pandas 2.2.2 and numpy 1.26.4. To confirm the reported numbers survive a clean install, all seven domain scripts were re-run from scratch in a fresh virtual environment built from that file. Every metric value reported in Section 5 reproduced exactly, 126 in total, covering baseline and post-constraint demographic parity difference, equalized odds difference, accuracy or AUC, and the disparate impact ratios of Section 5.4.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Baseline Model Performance

No single model dominates baseline performance. Among the five evaluations where true classification accuracy is available, GradientBoosting achieves the highest accuracy in three (Folktables, 0.845; Student, 0.658; MEPS, 0.992) while LogisticRegression leads in the other two (COMPAS, 0.686 vs. GradientBoosting's 0.674; FairGround's law_school_lequy sub-dataset, 0.913 vs. 0.910). Student shows the lowest accuracy of these five, followed closely by COMPAS, consistent with the added difficulty of COMPAS's six-group racial classification and Student's comparatively small dataset. RandomForest is never the top performer in any domain, in either the accuracy-reporting or AUC-reporting group.

The remaining three domains report AUC rather than accuracy, since their Stage 2 pipelines do not compute an accuracy score for the baseline model. GradientBoosting leads all three here as well: Law School (0.878) and Lending Club (0.712) both show only a small gap over LogisticRegression (0.872 and 0.706 respectively), while Agricultural shows by far the largest LR-GB gap of the group (0.938 vs. 0.727) alongside the highest AUC observed across the full study. Accuracy and AUC are not comparable, so the two groups are ranked separately.

### 5.2 Post-DP Constraint: DPD Results

Applying the demographic parity constraint produces model-dependent outcomes. FairGround's law_school_lequy sub-dataset shows the strongest result: all three models improve, led by gradient boosting's 95.9% reduction, with the other two comparable. Law School, which tests two models, is similarly strong at 91.5%.

Three domains show the constraint working against the intended goal for at least one model. Agricultural worsens under both models evaluated, consistent with a near-fair baseline leaving the optimizer nothing to correct. COMPAS improves only under gradient boosting, its other two models worsening to an identical 0.714. Folktables shifts slightly the wrong way under gradient boosting while its other two models move in opposite directions from each other.

MEPS, the healthcare domain, is the only evaluation whose baseline disparity falls between the two thresholds this study set. All three models improve and none worsens, gradient boosting most steeply at 78.2%.

Lending Club, near-fair at baseline, splits by model in the same way: gradient boosting improves modestly while logistic regression worsens slightly.

Student splits within a single domain: logistic regression and gradient boosting both improve while random forest worsens by more than half. This pattern, where models in one domain respond in opposite directions to the same constraint, recurs across the study and motivates the domain-and-model-specific framing in Section 6.

### 5.3 Post-EO Constraint: EOD Results

Under the equalized odds constraint, Law School and Student show the strongest results: both of Law School's models improve, gradient boosting by 98.7%, and all three of Student's improve, gradient boosting by 63.7%.

FairGround's two evaluated sub-datasets diverge sharply. The law_school_lequy sub-dataset, used for FairGround's headline comparisons elsewhere, shows all three models improving substantially under EO. The creditcard sub-dataset shows the opposite, all three worsening from baselines between 0.011 and 0.018, consistent with a near-fair baseline leaving the optimizer nothing to correct.

MEPS worsens slightly on all three models. Its gradient boosting baseline of 0.002 is close enough to zero that percentage change is uninformative: a 700% worsening in relative terms, 0.014 in absolute. Section 6.3 returns to this.

The remaining domains are mixed. COMPAS improves substantially under gradient boosting from a baseline of 1.000 but stays high at 0.659. Agricultural worsens under both models. Folktables improves under logistic regression alone. Lending Club barely moves in either direction.

### 5.4 Disparate Impact Ratio

Disparate impact ratio is computed per domain using each domain's own sensitive-attribute groups and is not aggregated, since the compared groups differ by domain. Law School's GradientBoosting result moves DIR from 0.643 to 0.957, crossing the commonly cited four-fifths (80%) threshold after intervention. This threshold originates in the EEOC's Uniform Guidelines and is applied here as an empirical heuristic rather than a binding legal standard for lending or criminal-justice contexts; compliance requirements vary by domain and jurisdiction. Lending Club's baseline ratio is 2.778 for gradient boosting and 1.832 for logistic regression, both above 1.0, meaning the lowest income quartile is selected substantially more often than the highest rather than less. The model amplifies the disparity present in the data, which runs at roughly 1.4x, to a predicted 2.8x. The constraint brings gradient boosting to 0.973 and logistic regression to 0.992, close to parity from the other side. Agricultural's GradientBoosting overcorrects, moving DIR from 0.653 to 1.042, past the point of parity rather than toward it. Folktables has a baseline DIR of 0.54, below the 0.8 line, but its script computes no post-constraint value, so it cannot appear in the before-after comparison. COMPAS, FairGround, and Student compute no DIR at all, which is distinct from a below-threshold result. DIR surfaces this Agricultural overcorrection in a way DPD alone does not, since DPD's raw difference does not distinguish a model that has swung past fairness from one still short of it.

![Figure 3: DP and EO improvement ranking across seven evaluations](../figures/stage2/cross_domain_improvement_ranking.png)

Folktables (DP) and Agricultural (both constraints) are the counterproductive exceptions; all other domains show positive improvement.

Considered together, the results support an empirical pattern rather than a universal rule for when ThresholdOptimizer helps. Counting every model-domain pair the study evaluated, rather than one representative model per domain, the constraint improved DPD in 9 of the 14 pairs whose baseline DPD exceeded 0.2, and worsened DPD in 3 of the 4 pairs whose baseline fell below 0.05. Three of those 14 high-baseline pairs come from law_school_lequy, which Section 6.4 shows is evaluated twice. Counting it once gives 6 of 11 or 7 of 12 depending on which pipeline is retained; the near-fair count is unaffected. Read at the domain level using gradient boosting, the same pattern holds with different arithmetic: effective in four of five high-baseline domains, with Folktables the exception, and counterproductive in one of two near-fair domains, with Lending Club the exception. The pair-level count is the more complete statement, since it uses every measurement rather than one model's.

MEPS occupies the range between the two thresholds, baseline DPD 0.110 to 0.116, which no other evaluation reaches. All three of its model-domain pairs improved. That is the only evidence this study offers about the middle of the range, and three pairs from a single data source cannot establish a third threshold; it is reported as an observation that the untested middle behaved like the high-disparity group rather than the near-fair one, and as a target for future work.

Effectiveness varies by model within a domain as much as it varies across domains: COMPAS's GradientBoosting improves under DP while its LogisticRegression and RandomForest both worsen, and the reverse pattern appears elsewhere. No single model dominates across every domain and constraint. GradientBoosting is not uniformly the strongest baseline performer, but it is consistently the most aggressive under constraint, producing both the largest fairness gains and, in FairGround, the largest accuracy cost (0.159).

### 5.6 Accuracy-Fairness Tradeoff

Accuracy cost is not computable for three domains. Law School, Lending Club, and Agricultural report AUC rather than classification accuracy, so no before-after cost can be derived from existing output, a scope limitation of those scripts. Law School's 91.5% DPD improvement is the largest among domains without a computable accuracy cost, though FairGround's 95.9% is larger overall.

Among the five evaluations where accuracy cost is computable, FairGround shows the clearest tradeoff: gradient boosting's largest fairness gain carries its largest accuracy cost (0.159), and logistic regression pays 0.148 for a smaller gain. Student's gradient boosting and random forest each cost 0.076 alongside real improvement. COMPAS and Folktables cost less, and COMPAS's gradient boosting is the one case where constrained accuracy slightly exceeds baseline. FairGround's random forest is a counterexample to the idea that a large fairness gain must be paid for: it cuts DPD from 0.336 to 0.012 at an accuracy cost of 0.010, a fraction of what the other two models in the same domain pay. No single model is cheapest across every domain; the lowest-cost model depends on which domain and which constraint.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 The Effectiveness Threshold Finding

Across the eight evaluations, the relationship between a domain's baseline disparity and ThresholdOptimizer's effect on that disparity is a strong empirical pattern rather than a strict rule. Domains with substantial baseline unfairness generally show large DPD and EOD reductions after the constraint is applied; COMPAS, Law School, and FairGround are the clearest examples. Folktables is a documented exception within this group: despite a high baseline DPD (0.320), GradientBoosting's post-constraint DPD moved slightly in the wrong direction (0.320 to 0.339). Agricultural sits at the opposite end and is the clearest exception overall. Its baseline DPD is 0.009, near-parity by any conventional threshold, yet ThresholdOptimizer does not leave it undisturbed: the intervention overshoots, and post-constraint disparity is measurably worse than doing nothing. Lending Club, also low-baseline-DPD, moves in the opposite direction from Agricultural, improving modestly under the same constraint. These exceptions are why the pattern is a decision heuristic rather than a guarantee: a constraint designed to reduce disparity can introduce it where none existed, because the optimizer solves for a target it assumes needs correcting, yet the same baseline-magnitude signal that predicts this in most domains does not predict it in all.

The practical implication is that baseline DPD should be audited before a post-processing constraint is applied, not after. A practitioner applying ThresholdOptimizer uniformly across a portfolio, without checking whether each model's baseline disparity warrants it, risks Agricultural's outcome: introducing the exact harm the constraint exists to prevent. Based on the pattern observed across all eight evaluations, we propose a decision heuristic as a starting point for practitioners, not a universal threshold: baseline DPD above 0.2 predicted effective intervention in 9 of 14 model-domain pairs, or 6 of 11 to 7 of 12 once the duplicated evaluation is counted once (Section 5.5), and baseline DPD below 0.05 predicted counterproductive intervention in 3 of 4. Given this exception rate, the heuristic should guide where to look rather than replace checking each model's post-constraint result before deployment; near-fairness or high disparity at baseline can reflect a genuine model property or a measurement artifact, and only a post-constraint check distinguishes them.

### 6.2 Multi-Metric Tradeoffs

Chouldechova's impossibility result, that demographic parity and equalized odds cannot generally be satisfied simultaneously when base rates differ across groups, is visible directly in this paper's own results. Improving DPD in several domains coincides with a smaller, and in some cases negative, improvement in EOD, confirming empirically what the impossibility theorem predicts analytically. A paper that reported only DPD, the metric practitioners most often default to, would present these domains as unambiguous successes and miss the tradeoff entirely.

DIR makes this concrete in a way DPD alone cannot. Agricultural's post-constraint DIR under gradient boosting rises to 1.042, which on its own reads as a favorable outcome, a selection rate for the previously disadvantaged group now marginally exceeding that of the advantaged group. Read alongside Agricultural's DPD overshoot from Section 6.1, the same result reads differently: the constraint has not found a fair equilibrium so much as swung past one, correcting an already-small disparity into a disparity of comparable size in the opposite direction. Reporting DIR and DPD together is what makes this overcorrection visible; a paper reporting one metric would show a clean success by its own terms while missing what the other reveals.

COMPAS surfaces a related but distinct version of the same tension. With six racial categories in the underlying data, no single post-processing threshold can simultaneously equalize outcomes across every pairwise group comparison; improving the gap between any two groups can widen the gap between a different pair. This is not specific to ThresholdOptimizer; it is the impossibility result manifesting whenever an intervention must satisfy more than two groups at once. FAPE's multi-metric reporting does not resolve this tension; no post-processing method can. But it makes the tradeoff visible to the practitioner making the deployment decision, rather than hidden inside a single aggregate number.

### 6.3 Production Monitoring Implications

The fairness improvements documented in Section 5 describe a single point in time: the moment a domain's model was evaluated immediately after the constraint was applied. Section 5.7's drift-detection results (Figure 4) show that this moment does not persist unmonitored. Under simulated distribution shift, the domains where ThresholdOptimizer produced the largest gains are also the domains where CUSUM registers the earliest drift alerts: Law School, FairGround, and Student. Gain and fragility appear related: a large correction from a substantial baseline disparity is also a large distance for drift to erode, so the domains with most room to drift are those that drift soonest.

Lending Club and Agricultural, both near-fair at baseline, trigger no CUSUM alerts under the same simulated shift, which is consistent with this pattern rather than a separate finding: there is comparatively little post-constraint gain in either domain for drift to erode. MEPS sharpens what 'gain' means here. Its constraint reduced DPD by 78.2%, proportionally among the largest reductions in the study, yet it also triggers no alerts. The absolute correction was small, 0.110 to 0.024, so little absolute distance exists for drift to erode even though the proportional gain was large. What predicts drift sensitivity in these results is the size of the correction in absolute terms, not the percentage improvement. Taken together, these results argue against treating a fairness audit as a one-time gate passed at deployment. The domains whose fairness compliance is most valuable to preserve, because the intervention did the most work there, are also the domains a static audit is least equipped to protect once the model or its input distribution changes after deployment. We therefore recommend that CUSUM-based monitoring, or a comparable alternative, accompany any post-processing fairness intervention in production, particularly where the intervention's absolute effect was large. This follows from a synthetic shift rather than observed production drift, and validating it against real deployment data is necessary future work.

### 6.4 Limitations

Agricultural lending, evaluated here via SBA 7(a) small business loan data with business type as the sensitive attribute, occupies a regulatory and substantive category distinct from most other domains in this study. COMPAS, Folktables, Law School, and Student concern discrimination against individuals along demographic lines (race, sex). Lending Club uses income band, a non-demographic proxy attribute, but remains a consumer-credit context governed by ECOA's individual-applicant provisions. Agricultural is the only domain concerning discrimination against business entities rather than individual consumers, governed by ECOA's business-credit provisions specifically. The distinction is substantive: business-type discrimination raises different fairness questions than either demographic or income-proxy discrimination, since business type is neither an immutable personal characteristic nor a proxy for one in the way income band can be.

Agricultural's inclusion is also methodologically load-bearing for this paper's central empirical claim. Agricultural has the lowest baseline disparity in the study, 0.005 for logistic regression and 0.009 for gradient boosting, making it the clearest test case for whether ThresholdOptimizer helps or harms an already near-fair system. The constraint is counterproductive here, moving DPD from 0.009 to 0.031 instead of toward parity, and this is the domain that most directly demonstrates the paper's core finding that intervention effectiveness depends on baseline disparity magnitude. Without a near-fair domain in the study, this half of the empirical pattern could not be shown.

Healthcare enters this study only through MEPS Panel 19, a survey-based dataset with race as the sensitive attribute, rather than through clinical records. MIMIC-III was identified during design as the domain where Obermeyer et al. (2019) documented the most consequential known fairness failure mechanism in production ML, and a data loader for it was built in anticipation of inclusion. Access requires PhysioNet credentialed registration, which was not granted before this paper's writing began. This is an access limitation rather than a design decision. MEPS gives the study a healthcare evaluation but a survey-based one; extending FAPE to clinical records once access is available remains a direct next step.

FAPE evaluates the constraint against each classifier separately rather than against a combined ensemble. Whether a post-processing constraint behaves differently on an ensemble than on its components is a question this design cannot answer and a direct extension of it.

Two of the eight evaluations draw on the same data. The standalone Law School domain and FairGround's reported sub-dataset are both law_school_lequy from the FairGround corpus, selected by that identifier in lawschool_loader.py and in the FairGround pipeline respectively, which is why both report 18,692 records. FAPE therefore covers seven independent data sources across eight evaluations, and the domain count should be read that way.

The two evaluations are not redundant, but what separates them is preprocessing rather than data. They use different feature construction and different sensitive-attribute definitions, since the FairGround pipeline adds sex alongside race and evaluates three models where the standalone pipeline evaluates two. The resulting numbers diverge modestly: baseline DPD under gradient boosting is 0.351 in the standalone pipeline and 0.342 in the FairGround pipeline, with post-constraint values of 0.030 and 0.014. Read as a pair, they give a small unplanned measurement of how much preprocessing choices alone move these metrics on identical data, which is a useful thing for a practitioner to see but is not a cross-domain result and is not counted as one.

## 7. Conclusion

One-time, single-domain fairness audits are the default practice in production ML, and this paper's results suggest both defaults are unsafe. Evaluating ThresholdOptimizer across eight evaluations spanning seven independent sources rather than one dataset in isolation surfaces a pattern invisible to any single-domain study: the intervention's effectiveness is not fixed, but depends on a domain's baseline disparity magnitude, and that dependence has real, documented exceptions rather than following a clean threshold. Agricultural lending's near-fair baseline (DPD 0.009) is the clearest demonstration that applying a fairness constraint without first checking whether it is warranted can introduce the exact harm the constraint exists to prevent.

The drift-detection results reinforce the same caution from a different angle. The domains where the intervention achieved the most, and where preserving that gain matters most, are also the domains where simulated post-deployment shift is detected earliest. A fairness audit performed once at launch says nothing reliable about six months later, just as an audit performed on one domain says nothing reliable about a different deployment context.

FAPE does not resolve the impossibility results Chouldechova and others have established; no post-processing method can. What it offers instead is a practical decision framework: audit baseline disparity before intervening, report multiple metrics rather than one, and monitor continuously rather than once. Several limitations bound these claims, most notably that the drift results are proof-of-concept on synthetic shift rather than observed production data, and that healthcare, the domain where the most consequential known fairness failure mechanism has been documented, remains excluded pending data access. Extending FAPE to a healthcare domain once access is available, and validating the drift-monitoring recommendation against real deployment data, are the most direct next steps this work identifies.
